Dee's Map of Knowledge

In 1570 the printer John Day published Henry Billingsley's translation of Euclid's Elements, the first in English. Dee wrote its long Preface "at my poore House At Mortlake." It is his most influential English work, written for "vnlatined people," craftsmen and practitioners rather than university scholars. Its argument is that mathematics is the key to almost every useful art, and to understanding God's creation.

At the end Dee gives the Groundplat, the "ground plan" of the whole discourse, printed as a fold-out tree. It starts from the two pure sciences, Arithmetic (number) and Geometry (magnitude). From them it branches into practical measuring and then into nineteen arts with names of their own, from Perspective and Astronomy to mining, clockmaking, navigation and the making of marvels. It ends with Archemastrie, the master art of experiment.

Behind it lies Dee's division of everything that exists into three kinds:

"All thinges which are, & haue beyng, are found vnder a triple diuersitie generall. For, either, they are demed Supernaturall, Naturall, or, of a third being."

Mathematical things are that third kind, standing between the divine and the natural. That is why the Groundplat says mathematics can be applied in two directions: Ascending to divine things, and Descending to natural ones.

    Practice: Dee's Arts by Hand

    Dee wrote for people who would use these arts. Each tool below turns one branch of the Groundplat into a short exercise you can do today.

    Musike · The Monochord

    The old way of teaching harmony: one string, a movable bridge. Shorten the string by a simple ratio and you get a consonance. Play the whole string, then the stopped part, then both together.

    Hypsometrie · Height by Shadow

    "How high … any thing is." On a sunny day, measure your own height and your shadow, then the shadow of a tree or tower. The two shadows keep the same proportion as the two heights.

    Apomecometrie · Distance by Thumb

    "How farre, from the Measurer, any thing is." Hold your thumb up at arm's length. Close one eye, then the other: the thumb seems to jump sideways. Estimate how wide that jump is at the far object, for example two car-lengths. Your arm is about ten times as long as the distance between your eyes, so the object is about ten times the jump away.

    Horometrie · True Noon

    "the precise, vsuall denomination of time … for any place assigned." Clock time is not Sun time. Find when the Sun is due south (north in the southern hemisphere) where you are today. At that moment a stick's shadow is at its shortest, so you can mark a noon line on the ground.

    Legacy: Archemastrie and the Rosicrucians

    The Preface ends with Archemastrie, an art that "teacheth to bring to actuall experience sensible, all worthy conclusions." Dee hints at more: the chief science of the Archemaster "is an other (as it were) OPTICAL Science," whose name he promises to give "when I shall haue some, (more iust) occasion."

    The historian Frances Yates argued that this practical, mechanical and optical "mathematical magic" was one source of the Rosicrucian programme of reformed learning announced in 1614–16. On this view, the Preface and the Monas Hieroglyphica were the two works of Dee's that carried his thought to Germany. Others, including Nicholas Clulee, have shown how much of the Preface is conventional Renaissance mathematics and caution against reading too much into it. Either way, it is Dee in his own words: confident that number and measure lead from the workshop to the divine.

    The Preface is also where Dee answers those who called him a conjurer for making mechanical marvels. His defence is in the Thaumaturgike section:

    "ought any honest Student, and Modest Christian Philosopher, be counted, & called a Coniurer?"
