Planetary Hours

Each day is divided into 24 planetary hours (12 day, 12 night). Each hour is ruled by a planet, starting with the day's ruling planet at sunrise.

How to Calculate:
  1. Find local sunrise and sunset times
  2. Divide daylight into 12 equal parts = day hours
  3. Divide night into 12 equal parts = night hours
  4. First hour after sunrise = ruling planet of that day
  5. Follow Chaldean order: Saturn→Jupiter→Mars→Sun→Venus→Mercury→Moon (repeat)
▶ Live Planetary Hours

Planetary hours are unequal: daylight from sunrise to sunset is split into 12 hours, and night from sunset to the next sunrise into another 12. In summer the day hours are long and the night hours short. The planetary day begins at sunrise, not midnight.

A short working by the hours:
  1. Choose the planet that matches your aim (see the planet boxes above).
  2. Find that planet's next hour in the table, ideally on its own day (for example, a Mercury hour on Wednesday for study or writing).
  3. Prepare beforehand so you can begin as soon as the hour starts. Hours can be shorter than 60 minutes.
  4. Open with grounding (see Energy Work), state your aim, do the work, then close and record the date, hour and result.
⌛ History & Sources: Astrology
  • The order Saturn → Jupiter → Mars → Sun → Venus → Mercury → Moon is the Chaldean order of Hellenistic astrology, which ranks the planets from slowest to fastest as seen from Earth.
  • Run the planetary hours in that order and the ruler of each day’s first hour gives the sequence of weekday names: Saturday, Sunday, Monday and so on. The Roman historian Cassius Dio (3rd century) explains the planetary week this way.
  • Unequal "seasonal" hours were the ordinary way of telling time in antiquity and the Middle Ages. Renaissance magic books such as Agrippa’s and the Key of Solomon time their operations by them.
  • The modern co-rulers (Pluto for Scorpio, Uranus for Aquarius, Neptune for Pisces) came only after those planets were discovered in 1930, 1781 and 1846. Traditional astrology uses Mars, Saturn and Jupiter.

Further reading: Ptolemy, Tetrabiblos (2nd c.); Cornelius Agrippa, Three Books of Occult Philosophy (1533); William Lilly, Christian Astrology (1647); Chris Brennan, Hellenistic Astrology (2017).

⌛ History & Sources: Deities
  • The domains and offerings above are modern devotional summaries. In the ancient world, worship of each god varied by city, century and cult, and each entry simplifies a long history.
  • Some offerings do have ancient roots. Thoth was the patron of scribes and linked with the Moon. Hecate received "Hecate’s suppers," food left at crossroads at the new moon.

Pendulum Dowsing

Simple yes/no divination using suspended weight.

How to Use:

  1. Hold pendulum still, establish "yes" (usually clockwise circle)
  2. Establish "no" (usually counter-clockwise or back-and-forth)
  3. Ask clear yes/no questions
  4. Observe movement for answer
Best For:

Quick answers, finding lost objects, checking energy of items, confirming tarot readings, testing food/supplements

Scrying

Gazing into reflective surface to receive visions.

Methods:

  • Black Mirror: Obsidian or painted glass, traditional method
  • Water Bowl: Dark bowl filled with water, simple and effective
  • Crystal Ball: Clear quartz or glass sphere, classic
  • Fire: Gazing into flames, pyromancy
  • Smoke: Watching incense smoke patterns

Technique:

  1. Dim lighting, candles preferred
  2. Enter light trance (soft focus, relaxed)
  3. Gaze into surface without straining
  4. Don't force visions - let them come
  5. Note symbols, images, impressions
  6. Ground after session

⌛ History & Sources: Divination
  • Tarot began in 15th-century Italy as a card game (tarocchi). It was first used for divination in the 1780s, by Etteilla in Paris. Antoine Court de Gébelin claimed an ancient Egyptian origin in 1781, but there is no evidence for it.
  • Most modern meanings descend from the Rider–Waite–Smith deck (1909), designed by A. E. Waite and drawn by Pamela Colman Smith. Waite swapped Strength and Justice to VIII and XI, the numbering used above.
  • Runes were an alphabet, used from about the 2nd century CE. The rune names are reconstructed from medieval rune poems in Old English, Norwegian and Icelandic. Rune divination is mostly modern, popularized by Ralph Blum’s Book of Runes (1982). Tacitus describes Germanic lot-casting with marked strips of wood, but he does not say the marks were runes.
  • Scrying has a well-documented history. John Dee’s obsidian mirror and crystal are in the British Museum.

Further reading: Michael Dummett, The Game of Tarot (1980); Decker, Depaulis & Dummett, A Wicked Pack of Cards (1996); R. I. Page, Runes (1987); Tacitus, Germania §10.

⌛ History & Sources: Energy Work
  • Chakras come from Indian tantric traditions, and the number and description of the centers vary from text to text. The seven-chakra model familiar in the West comes mainly from Sir John Woodroffe ("Arthur Avalon"), whose The Serpent Power (1919) translated the 16th-century Ṣaṭ-cakra-nirūpaṇa.
  • The rainbow colors (red root to violet crown) and the psychological keywords are 20th-century Western additions. The traditional texts give each center other colors, petals, letters and deities.
  • Grounding and shielding as practiced here are modern Western techniques, shaped by works such as Dion Fortune’s Psychic Self-Defence (1930). The LBRP comes from the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn (1888).
  • Rapid breathing to "raise energy" can cause dizziness or fainting. Do it sitting down, stop if you feel unwell, and avoid it if you have heart, lung or seizure conditions.

Further reading: Arthur Avalon, The Serpent Power (1919); Dion Fortune, Psychic Self-Defence (1930); Israel Regardie, The Golden Dawn (1937–40); Anodea Judith, Wheels of Life (1987).

Basic Meditation Techniques

Breath Awareness

Best For: Beginners, calming mind, grounding

  1. Sit comfortably, spine straight
  2. Close eyes or soft gaze downward
  3. Focus attention on breath
  4. Notice inhale and exhale naturally
  5. When mind wanders, gently return to breath
  6. Start with 5 minutes, build up

Body Scan

Best For: Relaxation, releasing tension, grounding

  1. Lie down or sit comfortably
  2. Bring attention to top of head
  3. Slowly move awareness down through body
  4. Notice sensations in each area
  5. Release tension as you go
  6. Continue to toes, then reverse back up

Mantra Meditation

Best For: Focus, spiritual connection, energy raising

  1. Choose a mantra (OM, deity name, affirmation)
  2. Repeat mentally or aloud
  3. Synchronize with breath if desired
  4. Let mantra fill your awareness
  5. Continue 10-20 minutes

Visualization Meditation

Best For: Astral work, manifestation, psychic development

  1. Enter relaxed state through breath work
  2. Visualize specific scene/object/symbol
  3. Build detail - colors, sounds, smells
  4. Interact with visualization
  5. Note any spontaneous changes
  6. Ground thoroughly after

Common Obstacles

"My mind won't stop thinking"

Solution: This is normal! Don't fight thoughts. Notice them, let them pass like clouds. The practice IS bringing attention back repeatedly.

"I can't visualize clearly"

Solution: Not everyone is visual. Try feeling, hearing, or knowing instead. "Visualization" can be any sensory imagination.

"I fall asleep"

Solution: Sit upright (don't lie down), meditate earlier in day, splash cold water on face first, or try walking meditation.

"I can't sit still"

Solution: Try walking meditation, yoga, or shorter sessions. Build tolerance gradually.

Meditation Tips:
  • Consistency matters more than duration - 5 min daily beats 1 hour weekly
  • Same time/place daily builds habit
  • Morning meditation sets tone for day
  • No "wrong" meditation - if you tried, you succeeded
  • Keep journal of experiences and insights

⌛ History & Sources: Meditation
  • Breath awareness is close to ānāpānasati, "mindfulness of breathing," which is taught in early Buddhist texts such as the Ānāpānasati Sutta.
  • The body scan as taught in the West comes from Vipassana practice, by way of Jon Kabat-Zinn’s Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction program (1979).

  • Not every herb here is safe to drink. Wormwood contains thujone, which is toxic in quantity. Do not make it into tea or tinctures without expert guidance.

  • Pets: many essential oils, including peppermint and other mints, are toxic to cats and dogs. Keep oils, sachets and smoke away from animals.
  • Smoke: burn incense and herbs with the window open. Smoke can trigger asthma.
  • Check with a doctor or pharmacist before ingesting herbs if you take medication. Herbs interact with drugs.
⌛ History & Sources: Plants
  • Assigning herbs to planets goes back to medieval and Renaissance herbal medicine. Nicholas Culpeper’s English Physitian (1652, later the Complete Herbal) gives a planetary ruler for almost every plant.
  • Most modern magical herb lists, including their elements and "magical uses," follow Scott Cunningham’s Encyclopedia of Magical Herbs (1985). Sources often disagree, so expect different rulers in different books.
  • Bay laurel’s link with prophecy is ancient: Apollo’s priestess at Delphi was associated with the laurel.

Further reading: Nicholas Culpeper, The English Physitian (1652); Scott Cunningham, Cunningham’s Encyclopedia of Magical Herbs (1985); Richard Mabey, Weeds (2010).

Shadow Work Reference

▼ Jump to the guided practice

What is Shadow Work?

Shadow work is the practice of exploring and integrating the parts of yourself you've repressed, denied, or hidden. The "shadow" (Carl Jung) contains rejected aspects of your personality - both "negative" traits you're ashamed of AND "positive" qualities you've suppressed.

Why Shadow Work Matters in Magic:
  • Unintegrated shadow leaks energy and sabotages spells
  • Repressed desires manifest unconsciously (often badly)
  • Facing shadow increases personal power
  • Honest self-knowledge = effective magic
  • Shadow contains hidden gifts and strengths

Shadow Work Techniques

Mirror Gazing

Intensity: High

  1. Sit before mirror in candlelit room
  2. Gaze into your own eyes for 10-15 minutes
  3. Don't look away even when uncomfortable
  4. Face may appear to shift, change, age
  5. Observe without judgment
  6. Journal immediately after

Reveals: Hidden aspects, alternate selves, suppressed emotions

Shadow Journaling Prompts

  • "What do I judge most harshly in others?" (This is often your shadow)
  • "What would I do if no one was watching?"
  • "What parts of myself do I hide from others?"
  • "What am I most ashamed of?"
  • "What do I secretly want but won't admit?"
  • "If I had no fear, I would..."
  • "The parts of me I've killed or buried are..."
  • "What would my 'dark side' say to me right now?"

Dialogue with Shadow Self

  1. Enter meditative state
  2. Visualize your shadow self appearing
  3. Ask: "What do you want me to know?"
  4. Allow it to speak (may be harsh or surprising)
  5. Listen without defending
  6. Ask: "What gift do you bring me?"
  7. Thank shadow self
  8. Journal conversation

Active Imagination

  1. Recall a disturbing dream or fantasy
  2. Re-enter it in meditative state
  3. Engage with dream figures
  4. Ask them what they represent
  5. Don't force answers - let unconscious speak
  6. Note any symbols or messages

Common Shadow Aspects

The Inner Critic

Harsh voice that judges and attacks you. Often internalized parent/authority.

Integration: Recognize it's trying to "protect" you from rejection. Thank it, but take back control.

The Victim

Part that feels powerless, wronged, helpless.

Integration: Acknowledge real hurts, but reclaim agency. You are no longer helpless.

The Saboteur

Undermines your success out of fear of change/responsibility.

Integration: Understand what it fears. Address those fears consciously.

The Tyrant

Desire for power, control, domination you've repressed.

Integration: Channel into healthy leadership, boundaries, assertiveness.

The Wild One

Primal desires, sexuality, rage, chaos you've suppressed.

Integration: Create safe containers for expression (ritual, art, controlled release).

Saturn Work (Astrological Shadow)

Saturn represents limitations, fears, karma, and lessons. Working with Saturn = shadow work.

Saturn Day Practice (Saturday)

  1. Identify a limitation or fear
  2. Light black candle
  3. Ask: "What is this teaching me?"
  4. Accept the restriction as teacher
  5. Work WITH limitation rather than against it

Shadow Integration Rituals

Dark Moon Shadow Release

Timing: Dark moon (3 days before new moon)

  1. Write down shadow aspects you've discovered
  2. Sit in darkness (no light)
  3. Read list aloud: "I acknowledge you. I accept you."
  4. Burn paper safely
  5. "I release shame. I integrate power."
  6. Sit in darkness, feeling wholeness

Shadow Box Spell

  1. Get black box
  2. Write shadow aspects on paper strips
  3. Add items representing them (mirror shard, black stone, etc.)
  4. Place in box: "I contain you, but you don't control me"
  5. Keep box on altar
  6. Open periodically to "check in" with shadow

Signs You Need Shadow Work

  • Projecting onto others (seeing in them what you deny in yourself)
  • Repeated self-sabotage
  • Intense emotional reactions (rage, shame, disgust)
  • Recurring nightmares
  • Feeling "stuck" or blocked
  • Magic backfiring or failing
  • Avoiding certain topics/situations intensely

After Shadow Work

Integration Practices

  • Art: Paint, draw, or sculpt shadow aspects
  • Movement: Embody shadow through dance
  • Voice: Sing or speak as your shadow
  • Writing: Let shadow write you a letter
  • Ritual: Create ceremony to honor integrated aspect

Grounding

Shadow work is INTENSE. Always ground after:

  • Eat heavy foods
  • Physical activity
  • Salt bath
  • Time in nature
  • Talk to trusted friend
Shadow Work Warnings:
  • Can bring up trauma - work with therapist if needed
  • Don't do alone if you have severe mental health issues
  • Go slowly - integration takes time
  • Not all dark thoughts = shadow (some are intrusive thoughts)
  • Integration ≠ acting out harmful desires
  • Some shadows are societal (racism, etc.) - don't integrate oppression
  • Not a substitute for therapy or medical treatment
The Goal:

Shadow work isn't about becoming "perfect" or eliminating the shadow. It's about making the unconscious conscious, so you have CHOICE. An integrated shadow becomes a source of power, creativity, and authenticity.

"Until you make the unconscious conscious, it will direct your life and you will call it fate." - Carl Jung

⌛ History & Sources: Shadow Work
  • The "shadow" is Carl Jung’s term for the parts of the personality that the conscious self does not acknowledge. He developed the idea most fully in Aion (1951).
  • The quotation above ("Until you make the unconscious conscious…") is a popular paraphrase. Jung’s own wording in Aion is close to: "When an inner situation is not made conscious, it happens outside, as fate."
  • The face "shifting" during mirror gazing has a known perceptual cause, the strange-face-in-the-mirror illusion. Giovanni Caputo (2010) found that most people see distortions after about a minute of gazing in dim light. Avoid the exercise if you have a history of dissociation or psychosis.

Further reading: C. G. Jung, Aion (1951), Collected Works vol. 9ii; Giovanni B. Caputo, "Strange-face-in-the-mirror illusion," Perception 39 (2010); Connie Zweig & Jeremiah Abrams, eds., Meeting the Shadow (1991).
