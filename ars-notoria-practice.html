✦ The Notory Art of Solomon ✦

A practitioner's exercise after Robert Turner's English translation of 1657

What the Notory Art Is

The Ars Notoria is a medieval Latin ritual text. Surviving manuscripts go back to the 13th century. It claims to be the art an angel taught King Solomon, handed down through a "Master Apollonius." Its promise is ambitious: by praying certain prayers and contemplating certain figures, called notae or "Notes", at set times of the Moon, you gain memory, eloquence, understanding and perseverance, and then mastery of the liberal arts, in a fraction of the usual time.

It was popular among students and clerics, and it was controversial. Thomas Aquinas rejected it as an illegitimate way to seek knowledge (Summa Theologiae II-II, q. 96, a. 1). The monk John of Morigny practiced it for years, came to fear it, and wrote his own Marian replacement for it, the Liber florum.

The only English version is Robert Turner's translation of 1657. He worked from a Latin text printed in the editions of Agrippa's works, and this site's copy is a transcription by Benjamin Rowe. Turner left out the figures. The manuscripts that do have them show at least three different sets, and no one set counts as definitive.

⚠ A note on method

The text is confusing: it mixes three sections and repeats or contradicts itself. This exercise draws its rules, its numbers and its prayers from Turner's text. The prayers are quoted in Turner's 1657 English. Where the text is unclear, we say so instead of guessing.

One open question: we don't know exactly what "inspecting" a Note meant. It may have meant memorizing the figure and seeing it in the mind's eye while reciting. The text itself says that someone without access to the figures can say each prayer twice instead.

⚠ Health

The text asks for fasting. It lets you off if you can't ("if thou canst not, then take another hour"). Skip the fast if it isn't safe for you.

The Shape of the Art

The book works from general to particular. The generals come first: faculties that every kind of learning needs. Then come the specials: Notes for each art, each with its own count, days and rules. Click any box.

Moon Calendar

Almost every instruction in the Ars Notoria is tied to the day of the Moon, that is, how many days have passed since the new moon. Below is the current lunation, worked out astronomically. Click a day to see what the text assigns to it, or pick a date.

Day

    Days count from the moment of the true new moon. Medieval practitioners often counted from the first sight of the crescent, which can be a day or two later. A lunation lasts 29 or 30 days, so day 30 doesn't always come. The text says some months are "more profitable" for certain arts by sign. We read that as the sign the new moon falls in, but the text doesn't say so explicitly.

    The Work: A Session

    One session in the order the text gives, using the first Note of Grammar as the example, since Grammar is where the specials begin. Plan on about an hour the first time.

    1 · Prepare

    Turner's text sets several conditions before anything is said or looked at:

    • Choose the day. Grammar's first Note belongs to the days "when the Moon is in her prime," days 1 to 17. Check the calendar.
    • Fast on the first day. "In the beginning of the inspection of all Notes, fast the first day till the evening, if you can; if thou canst not, then take another hour."
    • Keep it secret. "See that it be secret to thy self, and that thou have no looker on but God."
    • Live cleanly. The text repeatedly asks you to "abstain from sins" and to live "chaste and soberly" while using the Notes.
    • Keep the books of the art in front of you. For Logic and Rhetoric the text says to have the books of that art before your eyes. Doing the same for Grammar is a sensible choice.
    ◉ Before you begin
    Name, to yourself, what you want to learn and why. The Ars Notoria promises learning, not power. Its whole frame is a prayer for understanding.

    2 · The Opening Prayer

    "Before any one is to reade or pronounce any Orations of this Art … let them always first reverently and devoutly rehearse the Prayer in the beginning." This is the prayer the book opens with:

    The Prayer in the beginning · Turner 1657 Alpha and Omega! Oh Almighty God, the Beginning of all things, without Beginning, and without End: Graciously this day hear my Prayers; neither do thou render unto me according to my sins, nor after mine iniquities, O Lord my God, but according to thy mercy, which is greater then all things visible and invisible. Have mercy upon me, O Christ, the Wisdom of the Father, The Light of the Angels, The Glory of the Saints, The Hope, Refuge, and Support of Sinners, The Creator of all things, and Redeemer of all humane Frailties, who holdest the Heaven, Earth, and Sea, and all the whole World, in the palm of thy Hand: I humbly implore and beseech, that thou wilt mercifully with the Father, illustrate my Minde with the beams of thy holy Spirit, that I may be able to come and attain to the perfection of this most holy Art; and that I may be able to gain the knowledge of every Science, Art, and Wisdom; and of every Faculty of Memory, Intelligences, Understanding, and Intellect, by the Vertue and Power of thy most holy Spirit, and in thy Name. And thou, O God my God, who in the Beginning hast created the Heaven and the Earth, and all things out of nothing; who reformest, and makest all things by thy own Spirit; compleat, fulfil, restore, and implant a sound understanding in me, that I may glorify thee and all thy Works, in all my Thoughts, Words, and Deeds. O God the Father, confirm and grant this my Prayer, and increase my Understanding and Memory, and strengthen the same, to know and receive the Science, Memory, Eloquence, and Perseverance in all manner of Learning, who livest and reignest, World without end. Amen.

    3 · The First Precept: Hely Scemath

    Next comes what Solomon "entitled, His First Revelation; and that to be without any Interpretation." The text gives only its opening words and breaks off with "&c." Turner's text says it should be said "in the morning betimes of that day, wherein thou wilt begin any work."

    The first Precept · Turner 1657 Hely, Scemath Amazaz, Hemel, Sathusteon, hheli Tamazam, &c.
    ◉ Contemplate
    The text insists these words "cannot be expounded nor understood, by humane sense." Saying words you can't understand is an act of trust, not of study. Notice how it feels to say them without trying to work out what they mean.

    4 · The Speculum of Wisdom

    Turner calls this "the Oration of the four Tongues," also named the Splendor or Speculum of Wisdom, and "the Foundation of the whole Science." It belongs to particular Moon days (4, 8 and 12 especially). On those days it is said four times: in the morning, about the third hour, about the ninth hour, and in the evening. The third and ninth hours are counted from sunrise, so they fall around mid-morning and mid-afternoon.

    How to say it: "spoken secretly; and let him that speaks it be alone, and pronounce it with a low voyce, so that he scarcely hear himself."

    The Oration Assaylemath, Assay, Lemath, Azzabue.
    The second part, which is to be said only once Azzaylemath, Lemath, Azacgessenio.
    The third part, spoken together with the other Lemath, Sabanche, Ellithy, Aygezo.

    If today isn't one of its days, skip this stage. The calendar marks its days.

    5 · The Orations Before the Note

    "At the beginning of a Note, having seen the generals; let the specials be looked into." For the first Note of Grammar the text gives a short oration followed by a longer one:

    The first Oration at the beginning of the Note The Light, Truth, Life, Way, Judge, Mercy, Fortitude and Patience, preserve, help me, and have Mercy upon me, Amen.
    Said in the beginning of the first Note of Grammar Oh Lord, Holy Father, Almighty, eternal God, in whose sight are all the foundations of all creatures, and invisible beings whose Eyes behold my imperfections, of the sweetness of whose love the Earth and Heavens are filled; who sawest all things before they were made, in whose book every day is formed, and all mankind are written therein: behold me, thy Servant this day prostrate me before thee, with thy whole Heart and Soul: by thy Holy Spirit confirm me, blesse me, protect all my Actions in this inspection or repetition, and illuminate me of thy visitation.

    6 · Inspect the Note

    The rule for Grammar's first Note: "In the dayes when the Moon is in her prime, the first Note is to be looked into 12 times, and the Oration thereof repeated 24 times with Holy reverence; making a little space between, let the Oration be twice repeated at the inspection of every Note."

    In practice: look at the figure, say the oration twice, pause, and repeat twelve times over the day. On days 15 and 17 the first and second Notes are inspected 20 times and the oration said 30 times.

    ✎ Do
    Open the inspection view. It shows a manuscript figure with a counter for your inspections and repetitions. Rest your eyes on the whole figure first, then let them follow the lines of writing as they wind around it.

    7 · Close and Record

    Turner's text promises results "in one Moneth" to anyone who keeps the appointed times. Keeping a record is the only way to test that for yourself. After each session write down the day of the Moon, which Note you worked with, your counts, and anything you noticed. The text also mentions visions, so include dreams.

    ◉ Contemplate
    The book is called the Notory Art because "in certain brief Notes, it teacheth and comprehendeth the Knowledge of all Arts." What would it mean for a whole subject to be folded into one figure you can hold in your mind?

    Inspection

    Choose a figure and a rule. Press LOOKED each time you take in the figure, and SAID each time you finish the oration. Counts are saved for today only.
