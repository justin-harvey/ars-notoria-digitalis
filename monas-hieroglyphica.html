☿ The Hieroglyphic Monad ☿

A practitioner's exercise after Dr. John Dee's Monas Hieroglyphica (Antwerp, 1564)

What the Monas Is

In 1564 John Dee, mathematician and later advisor to Elizabeth I, published a short Latin treatise built around one symbol of his own invention. He dedicated it to the Emperor Maximilian II and said he had written it in about twelve days, as if the pen were being guided for him.

The symbol joins four familiar marks into one figure: the Moon ☽, the Sun ☉, the Cross of the elements, and the horns of Aries ♈, the sign of fire. Dee called it a monad: a single unit that contains the many. In twenty-four short theorems, laid out like Euclid's geometry, he builds the figure piece by piece and draws astronomy, alchemy, number and Kabbalah out of it.

The full title says how it is meant to be read: Monas Hieroglyphica, Mathematice, Anagogiceque explicata, "explained mathematically and anagogically" (anagogically means spiritually, leading upward). Dee used the glyph as his personal emblem for the rest of his life. Later alchemists and the Rosicrucians took it up, and it is still one of the best-known symbols in Western esotericism.

⚠ A note on method

Dee left no ritual and no step-by-step practice. His text is famously hard; the historian Frances Yates found it left the reader bewildered. The exercise below is a modern reconstruction. It follows the order in which Dee's theorems build the figure, and it turns each stage into drawing and contemplation. Wherever we report what Dee argued, we put it in our own words; the prompts for contemplation are ours.

⚠ Not Enochian

The Monas is not part of the Enochian "angelic" system Dee received through the scryer Edward Kelley in the 1580s. It came about twenty years earlier, and Dee wrote and published it himself.

Three Ways to Read the Figure

Scholars generally find three layers in the Monas. They overlap, and the exercise touches each one.

I. Contemplation

This is the core. You study the figure and the theorems until you can see how point, line and circle give rise to the Sun, Moon, elements and planets. Dee pictured knowledge as a climb from visible things to invisible ones. To him the symbols were not just pictures of truth but truth itself, so studying them was meant to change the person who studies.

II. The "Real" Kabbalah

Ordinary Kabbalah works on the letters of written Hebrew. Dee called that the vulgar Kabbalah and set against it a real one, which reads the structure of creation itself. In his eyes the Monas was a key to that deeper reading, an ancient art he was restoring rather than inventing.

III. Alchemy

The fire sign at the base and the figure as a whole point to alchemical operations. Take away the horns of Aries and what is left is the sign of Mercury ☿, the central substance of the alchemists. Several theorems read like veiled descriptions of laboratory work, and scholars still debate whether they mean real chemistry, an inner process, or both.

The Work: Building the Monad

Seven stages, in the order Dee builds the figure. For each one: read the teaching, draw that part by hand on your own page, then sit with the prompt. The figure on the left grows with you. Plan on 30 to 45 minutes. Dee meant the Monas to be studied again and again, so repeating the whole sequence over several sittings is part of the practice.

✎ Before you begin
  • Unlined paper, a pencil and a pen. A compass and a straightedge if you have them: Dee was a geometer, and the figure is built from true circles and right angles.
  • A quiet half hour. Leave room under your drawing for notes.
  • Place your page in portrait orientation. The figure is tall: crescent at the top, horns at the base.

1 · The Point ·

The teaching. Dee starts before there is any figure at all. A circle can't be drawn without a line, he argues, and a line can't be drawn without a point, so everything begins from the point. In the Monad the point is the Earth, the fixed center around which, in the cosmos of his day, the Sun, Moon and planets turned.

✎ Draw
Mark a single firm dot a little above the middle of your page. Everything else will be measured from it.
◉ Contemplate
Look at the dot for a full minute. It has position but no size. Notice that every line you draw tonight is already implied by where you put it. What is the fixed center your own life turns around?

2 · The Circle: the Sun ☉

The teaching. Dee's first theorem says the straight line and the circle are the first and simplest way to represent anything at all. Put a circle around the point and you have the ancient sign of the Sun. Dee gives the Sun the highest dignity: it is the ruler and king of the figure.

✎ Draw
Set the compass point on your dot and draw one full circle. Leave room above it for the crescent and plenty of room below for the cross.
◉ Contemplate
The point has turned into a boundary without moving. Center and circumference are now one sign. Think about the relation between a source and everything that radiates from it.

3 · The Crescent: the Moon ☽

The teaching. The Moon's crescent sits on top of the Sun. Dee admits this makes the Moon look superior, but says the reverse is true: the Moon only reflects the Sun's light, and she seems to long so much for that light that she vanishes from the sky for days before she returns. Joining Sun and Moon also recalls Genesis, where evening and morning together make the first day. For Dee this pair is where the "light of the philosophers" begins.

✎ Draw
Draw a crescent like a cup, horns pointing up, with its lowest point just resting on the top of the circle.
◉ Contemplate
What sits higher in you than it deserves to, shining with a light it doesn't make? And what quiet source actually feeds it?

4 · The Cross: the Elements ✝

The teaching. Sun and Moon now rest on a right-angled cross, and Dee reads it two ways at once:

  • As three, the ternary: two lines joined at one shared center. Dee links it to the old threefold division of body, soul and spirit.
  • As four, the quaternary: four arms enclosing four right angles. These are the four elements, Earth, Water, Air and Fire.

He also brings in the Pythagorean saying that 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 make 10: the point, the line, the triangle and the square together make a complete number.

✎ Draw
Drop a vertical line straight down from the bottom of the circle. Then draw a shorter horizontal bar across it, a little below the circle.
◉ Contemplate
Count the cross as three (two lines plus their meeting point), then as four (four arms). Go back and forth until both feel true at once. Where in your life is something both a single meeting and a fourfold spread?

5 · The Horns: Aries, Fire ♈

The teaching. The base of the figure is the sign of Aries, a fire sign and the first sign of the zodiac, where the year begins at the spring equinox. In alchemy fire is the agent of change. Putting Aries at the foot of the figure tells you that what sits above it is meant to be worked on and transformed, not just looked at.

✎ Draw
At the foot of the vertical line, draw two small arches, one curving off to each side, joined where the line ends.
◉ Contemplate
Fire starts things and fire refines them. What in you is ready to be heated, and what are you willing to have burned away?

6 · The Whole: Seven in One

The teaching. With the figure complete, Dee shows that the signs of all seven classical planets can be found inside it, so the one figure holds the whole heavens. Press each planet below to see the parts that make up its sign. Our breakdowns follow the standard astronomical glyphs. Dee's own derivations go into more detail.

Select a planet.
✎ Draw
On a fresh sheet, draw the whole Monad from memory in one sitting. Then go over each planet's parts in a different color, or point them out with your pencil.
◉ Contemplate
Notice that Moon, circle and cross together make Mercury ☿, the alchemists' key substance, and that the fire of Aries sits right underneath it. Read this way, the Monad is a picture of Mercury being worked by fire.

7 · Seal and Record

Dee meant the Monas to be studied over and over, one theorem at a time, with understanding coming slowly. Close each session by writing down what came up. Over several sittings, the record of what changes is the real result of the practice.

✎ Do
  1. Trace the figure one last time below, in the order you learned it: point, circle, crescent, cross, horns.
  2. Answer the prompts in the record.
  3. Next session, take just one stage and stay with it longer.
